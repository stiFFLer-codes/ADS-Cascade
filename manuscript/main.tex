\begin{abstract}
Classification systems are usually built by selecting a mechanism -- rules,
retrieval, a trained model -- before any measurement of whether that choice
fits the data. We ask whether historical decision consistency, measured
before deployment, can be used to select between qualitatively different
classification mechanisms. We test this with a pre-registered, 240-condition
synthetic factorial experiment (20 seeds $\times$ 6 nominal-consistency
targets $\times$ 2 lexical conditions) comparing exact-match rules against
fuzzy retrieval, under both a lexically clean condition and a controlled
lexical/surface-form perturbation. Realized historical consistency is
strongly correlated with each mechanism's own accuracy (Pearson $r>0.9$ in
both lexical conditions), but it does not predict which mechanism wins: a
frozen, pre-specified consistency-threshold decision rule agrees with the
empirically best-performing mechanism in 100\% of comparisons in one
consistency band and in 0\% of comparisons in another, because the empirical
winner is instead a constant function of the separately manipulated lexical
condition -- a variable the consistency signal is constructed to be blind to.
This finding is scoped to one synthetic generator, one lexical-perturbation
model, and one motivating (non-evidentiary) production case study; it is not
a deployment or generalization claim. We conclude that a design-time selector
built on historical consistency alone should not be used to predict which
mechanism will win. Whether a selector that also conditions on a measured
representation-stability signal would reliably predict which mechanism wins
is the direct next hypothesis this experiment motivates: no such selector was
designed, prototyped, or tested here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Real-World Motivation and General Problem}

A production accounting-classification system applies a frozen,
pre-specified decision rule to a measured historical-consistency statistic
before choosing which classification mechanism to build. In one production
deployment, that statistic was measured at 91.2\% and the rule selected a
rules-first, exact-match mechanism. In an independently generated synthetic
reproduction of the same pipeline, the analogous statistic was measured at
87.56\% and the same rule selected its embedding-based branch
(\texttt{EMBEDDING\_PRIMARY}) instead -- a different mechanism from the
lexical-similarity \texttt{retrieval} mechanism this paper's Experiment~1
tests (Section~3.3). No embedding-based mechanism is evaluated anywhere in
this paper. Both figures sit within a few percentage points of the rule's own 90\% threshold,
so the two runs' decisions differ not because the underlying systems differ
in kind, but because a small measurement difference happened to fall on
opposite sides of a single cutoff. We report this observation as the
motivation for the research question below, not as evidence for it: neither
run measured whether the mechanism it selected actually performed best, and
the production figures cited here are drawn from a confidential engagement,
not independently reproducible from this repository.

This single observation is an instance of a general pattern: classification
systems typically fix a mechanism class -- an exact-match lookup, a fuzzy
retrieval index, a trained model -- before any measurement of the data the
system will actually operate on. A statistic computed from historical
decisions could, in principle, inform that choice before a system is built
or deployed. Measuring data properties before choosing a system design is
itself a long-studied idea, not a contribution of this paper (Section~2). What
this paper studies is narrower: whether one specific such statistic --
historical label consistency -- actually predicts which of two qualitatively
different mechanisms should be chosen, and under what conditions that
prediction holds.

\subsection{Research Question}

The general problem of choosing among candidate procedures using measured,
historical evidence has an established name: the Algorithm Selection Problem
\citep{rice1976}, later unified with the meta-learning literature
\citep{smithmiles2009} and, more recently, reframed by the AutoML community as
automated workflow composition \citep{barbudo2023}. This paper operates at
the architecture/workflow level those literatures describe the field moving
toward, not at the per-instance level Rice originally posed. Section~2
develops this positioning fully; here we note only that the problem this
paper studies is a specific, narrower instance of a well-established research
question, not a new one.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/literature/terminology_map.md
% - research/literature/prior_art_map.md

The question this paper asks, precisely, is: can historical decision
consistency, measured before deployment, be used to select between
qualitatively different classification mechanisms (exact-match rules versus
retrieval-based fuzzy matching)? Answering it requires four preconditions to
hold in the target setting: a history of repeated decisions on the same or
similar items, observable ground-truth labels for those decisions, a
consistency statistic that can be measured from them (formalized in
Section~3.2 as the Automated Determinism Score, ADS), and sufficient
historical coverage for that statistic to be estimated reliably. This paper's
experiment is constructed to satisfy all four by design; where a real system
does not, the question this paper answers does not directly apply to it.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.11.A
% - STATE.md ("Literature review + paper-positioning conclusions")

The intuition motivating this question is straightforward. If a product has
almost always been booked to the same account historically, that looks like
strong evidence the mapping is already solved, and a simple, precedent-based
mechanism -- an exact lookup against history -- should suffice; conversely,
if the historical mapping is inconsistent, a more flexible mechanism should
be needed. The natural hypothesis this suggests is that higher historical
consistency should favor a simpler, precedent-based mechanism over a more
flexible one. This is exactly the hypothesis this paper tests, and, as
Section~5 reports, partly falsifies.

% EVIDENCE:
% - TECHNICAL_REPORT.md Sec.2.2 (framing only, not numbers)
% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.2 step 3

\subsection{Experimental Approach and Main Findings}

We test this with a pre-registered, 240-condition synthetic factorial
experiment. Two mechanisms are compared in isolation: exact-match rules and
rapidfuzz-based fuzzy retrieval (cutoff calibrated once, before the run, to
75). Two lexical conditions -- CLEAN (no surface-form perturbation) and
VARIED (a fixed, controlled lexical/surface-form perturbation) -- are crossed
with six nominal historical-consistency targets and twenty random seeds. A
third mechanism, a large language model, is deliberately excluded from the
primary comparison for a documented, principled reason detailed in
Section~4: not because it was inconvenient to include, but because the
synthetic generator's product string carries no signal predictive of the
true label, which would make any LLM result uninterpretable as evidence about
historical consistency.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.2, Sec.6, Sec.10

The experiment produces a two-part finding that this paper keeps explicitly
separate throughout. First, realized historical consistency strongly predicts
each mechanism's own accuracy: both the rules and the retrieval mechanism
become more accurate as consistency rises, in both lexical conditions.
Second, realized historical consistency does not predict \emph{which}
mechanism wins: the mechanism that actually performs best in a given
condition is instead a constant function of the (separately manipulated)
lexical condition, unrelated to how consistent the underlying data is. These
are two different claims, with two different (here, opposite) answers, and
this paper never merges them into a single statement about whether
"historical consistency works."

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.4, Sec.6

\subsection{Contribution Statement and Paper Roadmap}

This paper's contribution is a precise, evidenced boundary on what a
historical-consistency signal can and cannot tell a system designer, not a
new metric, a new architecture, or a validated selection method. Formally: in
this synthetic product-classification generator, realized historical decision
consistency is strongly predictive of each classification mechanism's own
accuracy (exact-match rules and fuzzy retrieval alike; Pearson $r\approx
0.91$--$0.96$ in both lexical conditions). It is \emph{not}, however,
predictive of which of the two mechanisms will outperform the other: under a controlled
lexical perturbation, retrieval wins on whole-set accuracy in every tested
noisy condition, and the two mechanisms are statistically indistinguishable
in every tested noise-free condition, in both cases independent of realized
consistency across its full observed range. A pre-specified,
consistency-only decision rule consequently agrees with the empirical winner
only in the sub-range where its own threshold-driven recommendation happens
to coincide with the constant true winner, because the consistency signal is
computed from a stable product identity that cannot observe the surface-form
instability that actually determines the outcome here. This is not a new
metric (Section~2), not a new architecture, and not a method we claim
selects the right mechanism -- it is a bounded empirical finding about where
one specific, already-used signal succeeds and where it fails.

% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.6 (exact wording to reuse)

Section~2 positions this work against the algorithm-selection, meta-learning,
AutoML, self-designed-systems, reject-option, learning-to-defer, and model-
cascade literatures. Section~3 defines the historical-consistency signal and

Section~4 describes the experimental design in full. Section~5 reports

\section{Related Work}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Cluster Purity and Majority-Vote Agreement}

The historical-consistency signal this paper studies, the Automated
Determinism Score (ADS, Section~3), is mathematically identical to an
existing, well-established quantity. Cluster purity
\citep{manning2008,amigo2009} is defined as
$\mathrm{purity}(\mathrm{cluster}_k) = \max_j(\text{count of class }j\text{
in cluster }k) / |\mathrm{cluster}_k|$ -- the identical closed-form
expression as ADS under the substitution "cluster" $\leftrightarrow$ "an
item's historical booking multiset." Independently, the raw majority-vote
agreement proportion used throughout the crowdsourcing and truth-inference
literature descending from \citet{dawidskene1979} is the same quantity under
a different name, applied to the same kind of repeated-labeling data. We
therefore make no claim that ADS is a novel metric -- this is a closed
question, not an open one, and the paper's contribution, developed in the
following subsections and in Section~5, lies entirely in what this
already-known metric is used for, not in its formula.

\subsection{Design-Time Algorithm and Architecture Selection}

The Algorithm Selection Problem \citep{rice1976} originates the general
question of choosing a procedure from historical evidence, selecting a single
algorithm per problem instance. \citet{smithmiles2009} unifies this framing
with meta-learning, and later work applies the same design-time,
historical-evidence-driven pattern to select a generic machine-learning
algorithm within the same broad application area, classification, that this
paper studies. We make no claim that design-time selection from historical
evidence is itself new -- this general pattern is well established. This
paper's narrower question is whether a \emph{label-consistency-specific}
signal, as opposed to generic statistical meta-features or benchmarked
performance search, predicts a \emph{qualitative mechanism-class} choice
(rules versus retrieval, not one machine-learning algorithm versus another),
and it reports precisely where that narrower claim holds and where it fails.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/literature/citation_ledger.csv rows B1-01..B1-05, B2-01, B2-10, B2-11
% - research/literature/contribution_status.md (C2, C2b)

\citet{barbudo2023} documents the field's shift from single-algorithm
selection toward automated, benchmarked-performance-driven workflow
composition, describing the field as having moved from the algorithm
selection problem toward the broader challenge of composing an entire
processing workflow -- the closest terminological echo of this paper's own
framing found in the surrounding literature. This paper's simple,
interpretable threshold rule is not offered as a competitor to that automated
search paradigm. The delta is one of evidence type and mechanism, not
performance: this paper studies a label-consistency signal driving a named,
interpretable threshold rule, not a benchmarked optimization search over a
combinatorial pipeline space -- a difference in kind, not a claim of
superiority.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/literature/citation_ledger.csv row B2-02
% - research/literature/prior_art_map.md (Tier 2, "Design-time architecture selection")

\citet{idreoskraska2019} describes the strongest non-machine-learning
structural analog to this paper's design-time framing: measure historical
evidence once, choose system composition before serving traffic. The
evidence type there is workload or query \emph{frequency}, not label
\emph{consistency}, and the literature's own thesis is continuous
self-adaptation, explicitly rejecting a one-time, human-gated design
decision -- the opposite temporal structure from the one this paper's
experiment tests. We do not claim that a one-shot, pre-deployment design gate
is itself a contribution; if anything, it is the position this literature
argues against, cited here as contrast rather than as an unsolved problem
this paper addresses. This paper's experiment is about a specific signal type
(label consistency) and its specific limitation (blindness to representation
instability), not a defense of the one-shot-gate design pattern in general.

% - research/literature/citation_ledger.csv rows B7-01, B7-05, B7-06

\subsection{Inference-Time Selection and Escalation}

\citet{chow1970} originates the single-threshold accept/reject decision on
live posterior confidence, formalized further by \citet{elyaniv2010}'s
risk-coverage framework and surveyed comprehensively by
\citet{hendrickx2024}. Nothing in this experiment is offered as a new
reject-option variant. This literature is not directly engaged by
Experiment~1 at all, because Experiment~1 tests a \emph{design-time}
selection rule applied once before deployment, not a \emph{runtime} reject
threshold applied per item at inference time; we cite it only to establish
that distinction, which this paper's scope sits firmly on the design-time
side of.

\citet{mozannarsontag2020} establishes the modern learning-to-defer
framework: a trained or rule-based function decides, per item, whether to
defer to a human, informed by historical decision data. We do not claim that
this experiment's mechanism-selection rule is an instance of learning to
defer. As with reject-option methods, this literature establishes the same

side of; the production system's runtime human-escalation cascade sits closer
to this literature, but that cascade is not evaluated in Experiment~1, which
studies only the isolated rules and retrieval mechanisms.

\subsection{Domain-Specific Practice}

\citet{jorgensenigel2021} empirically demonstrates, in the same application
domain (financial transaction-to-account classification), that a global
classifier generalizes far worse across companies than per-company models --
the same phenomenon the production system's own cross-company-consistency
threshold encodes, though reported there as a direct accuracy gap rather than
formalized into a named metric. Independent industry sources (accounts-payable
automation vendors, not peer-reviewed) \citep{kenfromfinance2025,peakflo2025,ramp2025} describe
similar informal historical-consistency-audit practice already in production use. We do not
claim that the application domain itself is unprecedented, or that no vendor
measures historical consistency before choosing a mechanism -- at least one
industry source \citep{kenfromfinance2025} (not peer-reviewed) directly contradicts that framing. The narrow delta that
survives is academic, not methodological: no peer-reviewed study of this
specific document-classification niche was found in the literature search
this paper's evidence base draws on, which is a modest, honest niche-novelty
observation, much weaker than any claim of methodological novelty, and not
this paper's scientific contribution.

\section{Problem Setting and Signal Definition}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Historical Product-Level Decisions and Account Labels}

The data object every mechanism and every signal in this paper operates on
is a repeated, historically observed mapping from an item -- a "product" --
to a label -- an "account" -- together with an occurrence count. This
formulation is domain-agnostic: nothing in the definitions below references
Romania, fiscal documents, or invoices specifically, even though the
motivating case study (Section~1.1) and the synthetic generator (Section~4)
both instantiate it in that domain. Studying this question in a given setting
requires the four preconditions named in Section~1.2 to hold: a history of
repeated decisions, observable labels for those decisions, a consistency
statistic that can be measured from them, and sufficient historical coverage
for that statistic to be estimated reliably.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_DATA_DICTIONARY.md ("Unit of observation")
% - STATE.md (four preconditions)

\subsection{The Automated Determinism Score (ADS)}

For an item $p$ with historical occurrence counts $c_1,\dots,c_k$ across its
$k$ observed labels, the Automated Determinism Score is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ads}
\mathrm{ADS}(p) \;=\; \frac{\max_i c_i}{\sum_i c_i}
\end{equation}

the empirical probability that a random historical occurrence of $p$ was
assigned its modal label. This is mathematically identical to cluster purity
\citep{manning2008,amigo2009} and to the raw majority-vote-agreement
baseline used throughout the crowdsourcing literature descending from
\citet{dawidskene1979} (Section~2.1); it is a descriptive statistic, not a
novel contribution of this paper. Two aggregates of ADS over a product
population are used later in this paper only as diagnostic covariates, never
as the primary independent variable: the occurrence-weighted mean (what a
random transaction actually experiences) and the unweighted mean (the
catalog's intrinsic learnability). The primary independent variable
throughout Sections~4--5 is a third quantity, \emph{realized} ADS,
introduced precisely in Section~4.3.

\subsection{Two Compared Mechanisms: Exact-Match Rules and Fuzzy Retrieval}

The first of the two mechanisms this paper compares, \texttt{rules}, is an
exact company-scoped string lookup against the historical training data,
falling back to an exact global lookup if no company-scoped match exists; any
hit is returned as the mechanism's answer, and no hit results in abstention.
There is no confidence threshold to calibrate for this mechanism -- matching
is binary, exact-match or no-match. Under the whole-set-accuracy metric used
throughout this paper (Section~4.7), an abstention counts as incorrect, with
no partial credit. Coverage -- the share of test items for which the
mechanism does not abstain -- is below 1.0 in every one of the 240 conditions
reported in Section~5, a real, reported asymmetry with the retrieval
mechanism below, not one this paper normalizes away. This isolated mechanism
is a simplified test-harness component built for Experiment~1; it is not the
shipped production cascade's first tier, which additionally applies
confidence gating and human-review escalation logic outside this
experiment's scope.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.11
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_DATA_DICTIONARY.md (rules_* columns)

The second mechanism, \texttt{retrieval}, applies a rapidfuzz-based
lexical-similarity match (the \texttt{WRatio} scorer), company-scoped then
global, as the \emph{primary} mechanism for every test item -- not as a
fallback gated behind a rules-miss. A match is accepted if its similarity
score meets a cutoff calibrated once, before the frozen run, on a dedicated
calibration dataset disjoint from every condition reported in Section~5
(Section~4.5); items below the cutoff abstain. Coverage is 1.0 in every one
of the 240 conditions -- this mechanism never abstains at the calibrated
cutoff. We deliberately call this mechanism "retrieval," not "embedding,"
because it is a lexical-similarity match over surface strings, not a
semantic embedding model: no embedding model was trained, downloaded, or
evaluated anywhere in this experiment, and describing it as
"embedding-primary" would misstate what was actually tested.

\subsection{Mechanism Accuracy vs. Mechanism Ranking}

This paper is organized around a distinction that Section~5 keeps in
separate subsections throughout: "ADS predicts mechanism accuracy" and "ADS
predicts mechanism ranking" are different claims, with different (here,
opposite) truth values, and this paper never merges them. The first is a
correlational claim about a single mechanism's own performance level as
realized consistency varies (Section~5.2); the second is a claim about
\emph{which} of the two mechanisms wins a head-to-head comparison
(Section~5.3). A signal can be strongly informative about the first while
carrying no information about the second, precisely if the quantity that
determines relative ranking is one the signal cannot observe -- which is
exactly what Section~5 reports.

\section{Experimental Design}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Research Hypothesis}

H1 (revised), stated and pre-registered before any data from the frozen run
existed: under controlled synthetic conditions, higher realized historical
decision consistency will be associated with predictable changes in relative
mechanism performance, such that a pre-specified, frozen consistency-based
decision rule (R3, defined in Section~4.7) will agree with the empirically
best-performing mechanism more often than chance. This phrasing is
deliberately weaker than an earlier draft that required monotonic dominance
in every consistency band; H1 claims only better-than-chance agreement, with
falsification criteria fixed in advance (Section~4.8).

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.2

\subsection{Synthetic Generator and Factorial Design}

Every condition is generated from a synthetic invoice-classification
generator spanning 60 companies and a 1,200-product vocabulary, fixed across
every condition. Each generation call draws its own independent,
reproducible random stream (\texttt{random.Random(seed)}) rather than
mutating one shared global generator, so that a 20-seed sweep produces
statistically independent replicates. Cross-company alignment -- the rate at
which different companies agree on a shared product's dominant account -- is
fixed at 0.695 -- the production-observed value, cited from a confidential
engagement and not independently reproducible from this repository -- so
that it does not become a second, uncontrolled independent variable
alongside the consistency sweep itself. This generator is not calibrated against real invoice or OCR error
data; Section~7 states this limitation explicitly.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.4, Sec.5

Two factors are varied systematically. The first is the generator's nominal
target for the share of products intended to be near-deterministic, run at
six levels: $\{0.00, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00\}$. The second is the
lexical condition, CLEAN or VARIED, two levels. Crossed with 20 seeds, this
produces $6 \times 2 \times 20 = 240$ conditions exactly, the frozen final
run this paper reports. A separate, wider 17-point grid was used only during
an earlier, mechanism-blind calibration pass (Section~4.8) to characterize
the generator's realizable range; it is not itself part of the 240-condition
frozen run and its data does not appear in Section~5.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_FINAL_RESULTS.md Sec.3
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_EVIDENCE_CHECKPOINT.md Sec.3

\subsection{Realized ADS vs. Target Deterministic Share}

The generator's nominal target is an input knob at generation time, not
itself the scientific independent variable. The independent variable this
paper actually reports against, throughout Section~5, is \emph{realized}
ADS -- the measured, train-only share of products whose per-product
determinism score exceeds 0.95, computed after generation, grouped by the
stable product identity. Realized ADS is the exact quantity the frozen
decision rule R3 (Section~4.7) thresholds; the two usually, but do not
always, coincide with the nominal target. At the target of 0.50, for
instance, individual seeds' realized ADS straddles R3's 0.70 boundary
(observed range 0.659--0.727 across 20 seeds), so seeds sharing one nominal
target can land in different R3 bands. Across the frozen run, realized ADS
ranges from 0.44 to 0.93, structurally capped below approximately 0.91 by the
fixed cross-company-alignment parameter (Section~4.2): the region of
realized ADS at or above roughly 0.93 was never reachable by this generator
and is therefore untested, not merely unfavorable. Realized ADS is invariant
to the lexical condition by construction, since it is computed by grouping on
the stable product identity, never on the perturbable surface string -- this
is confirmed directly in the data (byte-identical realized-ADS means and
standard deviations between the CLEAN and VARIED conditions at every target).
Every result, table, and figure in Section~5 uses realized ADS, never the
nominal target, as its independent-variable axis.

\subsection{Lexical Conditions: CLEAN and VARIED}

Under CLEAN, the generator's product surface strings are emitted without any
lexical perturbation -- the existing generator's unmodified default output.
Under this condition, the rules and retrieval mechanisms are statistically
indistinguishable (within a pre-registered practical-equivalence margin of
$\delta=0.02$ whole-set-accuracy points) at every one of the six targets:
zero of the 120 CLEAN conditions produce a defined empirical winner
(Section~5.3). This is evidence that the two mechanisms perform almost
identically specifically when there is no lexical noise for retrieval's
fuzzy matching to be robust to, for this generator -- not a general claim
that the two mechanisms are equivalent.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.8
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_FINAL_RESULTS.md Sec.4 (CLEAN rows)

Under VARIED, a fixed, five-transform perturbation algorithm is applied,
independently per invoice line, only to the display/lookup string; the
ground-truth product identity used to build the mechanisms' knowledge bases
and to score accuracy is untouched, so semantic identity is preserved by
construction, not by convention. The five transform types are case
variation, punctuation variation, token reorder, abbreviation, and whitespace
variation. Each line is perturbed with probability
$P_{\mathrm{TRANSFORM}}=0.3$, a value chosen once, before the frozen run, by
a pilot sweep against a target corruption-share acceptance band, not derived
from measured real-world OCR or typo error rates and never re-tuned after
seeing mechanism-accuracy results. The perturbation is fully reproducible: a
deterministic per-line sub-seed derivation reproduces the identical transform
decision on any re-run of the same seed.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.8
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_CALIBRATION_REPORT.md (P_TRANSFORM calibration)

\subsection{Mechanisms and Retrieval Cutoff}

Only the retrieval mechanism has a tunable parameter, its similarity cutoff;
the rules mechanism has none. The cutoff was calibrated once, on a dedicated
generation run with its own seed, distinct from every seed used in the
240-condition frozen sweep, before that sweep was run. Candidate cutoffs
$\{70,75,80,85,90\}$ were evaluated against a product-identity hit-rate
criterion -- whether the top fuzzy match resolves to the same underlying
product as the query, not whether the resulting account label is correct --
subject to a minimum coverage floor of 30\%; a cutoff of 75 was selected and
frozen for the entire run, never re-tuned per band, per lexical condition, or
after seeing accuracy results.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.12
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_CALIBRATION_REPORT.md

\subsection{Seed Structure, Train/Test Separation, and Winner Definition}

Twenty seeds, $\{31001,\ldots,31020\}$, are used identically across every one
of the twelve (target, lexical) cells, for 240 total conditions. The seed
range is a documented, deterministic rule, fixed before the run and never
hand-picked after seeing any result.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_FINAL_RESULTS.md Sec.3
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_EVIDENCE_CHECKPOINT.md Sec.3

Realized ADS is computed exclusively from each condition's train split,
mirroring real deployment temporal logic: a design-time decision can only see
history, never the held-out future it will later be judged against. Each
mechanism's knowledge base is likewise built only from that condition's train
rows. Every accuracy and coverage number reported in Section~5 is computed
exclusively on that condition's test rows. This separation is verified in
code, not merely asserted: the consistency computation filters to train-split
rows before any aggregation, and no test-split label ever reaches the
mechanism-selection rule.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.13
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_EVIDENCE_CHECKPOINT.md Sec.11 (integrity checks)

Within a condition, both mechanisms are scored on identical held-out test
lines -- a paired, not an independent, comparison. The empirical winner is
defined by a paired bootstrap on the accuracy difference
$\mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{rules}}-\mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{retrieval}}$ (2{,}000
resamples, 95\% percentile interval): rules wins if the entire confidence
interval lies above $+\delta$; retrieval wins if it lies entirely below
$-\delta$; otherwise the condition is scored a tie, a distinct category never
broken toward either mechanism. The practical-equivalence margin
$\delta=0.02$ was fixed at this calibration stage, before the frozen run, and
is not re-examined against the outcome data anywhere in this paper.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:winner}
\mathrm{winner} =
\begin{cases}
\text{rules} & \text{if } \mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{rules}} - \mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{retrieval}}) \text{ entirely} > +\delta \\
\text{retrieval} & \text{if } \mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{rules}} - \mathrm{acc}_{\mathrm{retrieval}}) \text{ entirely} < -\delta \\
\text{tie} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

% calibration Gate 3). Standard practical-equivalence testing, not a
% methodological contribution of this paper.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

The primary performance metric for each mechanism is whole-set accuracy: a
prediction counts as correct only if the mechanism both answered and was
right, with abstention counted as incorrect and no partial credit. The
agreement rate between the frozen decision rule R3 (below) and the empirical
winner is the number of conditions where R3's selected mechanism equals the
empirical winner, divided by the number of conditions with a \emph{defined}
comparison -- excluding ties and conditions where R3 selects the excluded
LLM band. Because the LLM mechanism is excluded from the primary comparison,
R3 discriminates between exactly two mechanisms wherever a comparison is
defined, so the chance baseline used throughout this paper is 0.5, not
one-third. Agreement rates are tested against this chance baseline with an
exact two-sided binomial test, and reported with a Wilson 95\% confidence
interval.

The frozen decision rule R3 -- so named in the production system's own
internal rule taxonomy, not an alternative considered within this
experiment -- is reused unmodified from that system's architecture-decision
procedure and is not re-derived or re-tuned for this experiment. It maps
realized ADS to a mechanism recommendation by a fixed threshold: at or above
0.90, rules; between 0.70 and 0.90, retrieval; below 0.70, the excluded LLM
band.

\subsection{Calibration and Preregistration}

Three calibration gates were closed before the frozen run. First, a
mechanism-blind ADS calibration swept a dense 17-point target grid with ten
seeds per target -- using no import of, and no reference to, either
mechanism's code -- to characterize the generator's realizable realized-ADS
range and to choose the six final targets by a mechanism-blind greedy
furthest-point selection over that curve, so that no target band was chosen
with foreknowledge of mechanism behavior. Second, the retrieval cutoff was
calibrated as described in Section~4.5. Third, the empirical-winner, tie, and
confidence-interval definitions (Section~4.6) were fixed. The 17-point
calibration grid is wider than, and distinct from, the six-target grid
actually used in the 240-condition frozen run (Section~4.2); its own data
does not appear in Section~5.

% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_CALIBRATION_REPORT.md Sec.1-2

The falsification table used to classify the experiment's outcome
(H1~SUPPORTED / PARTIALLY~SUPPORTED / NOT~SUPPORTED / INCONCLUSIVE) was fixed
in advance of the frozen run. The observed pattern matches the pre-registered
PARTIALLY~SUPPORTED row exactly (Section~6.3), which is not a post-hoc
reinterpretation of the data but one of four possible outcomes specified
before any data existed. No threshold, mechanism definition, retrieval
cutoff, winner definition, or generator parameter was changed after the
frozen run's results were known; the post-hoc analysis this paper's Results
section also draws on explains the frozen data, it does not regenerate or
re-tune it.

The entire generator, both mechanisms, the lexical perturbation model, and

\subsection{ADS Does Not Predict Mechanism Ranking}

Realized ADS does not predict which mechanism wins. Under VARIED, the
empirical winner is retrieval in all 120 of 120 conditions -- exceptionless,
unconditional on realized ADS across its full observed range (0.44--0.93).
Under CLEAN, the empirical winner is a tie in all 120 of 120 conditions --
also exceptionless. The lexical condition here is a manipulated, controlled
experimental factor, not merely an observational correlate, which is what
licenses describing the empirical winner as \emph{governed by} the lexical
condition in Section~6, rather than only correlated with it. Because realized
ADS is invariant to the lexical condition by construction (Section~4.3), and
the empirical winner is a constant function of the lexical condition, ADS
structurally cannot track the quantity that actually determines the winner
in this experiment.

\subsection{R3 Threshold Agreement by Realized-ADS Region}

This subsection reports the paper's headline finding, leading with the band
structure rather than the flat aggregate. Binned by each row's own realized
ADS against R3's own thresholds -- the primary framing, matching the locked
wording this paper draws on -- R3 agrees with the empirical winner
in 100\% (32 of 32) of conditions in the realized 0.70--0.90 band, and in 0\%
(0 of 18) of conditions in the realized $\geq0.90$ band. A secondary, also

explicitly labeled, rather than silently switching between them.

Both individual by-nominal-target bands are far more statistically
significant than the flat aggregate ($p=1.9\times10^{-9}$ for the 30/30

informative bands (30 and 20 by the by-target framing; 32 and 18 by the

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

Realized ADS strongly predicts each mechanism's own accuracy (Section~5.2);
it does not predict which mechanism wins (Section~5.3); the mechanistic
reason is the ADS-by-representation-stability interaction reported in
Section~5.5; and the flat 64.0\% aggregate agreement rate alone
under-communicates the underlying 100\%-versus-0\% band structure
(Section~5.4). These four statements, taken together, are what Section~6
discusses; none of them is new information beyond what Sections~5.2--5.6
already established.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{What the Experiment Supports}

In this synthetic experiment, historical decision consistency is informative
about classification-mechanism \emph{difficulty}, not mechanism
\emph{ranking}, when, as observed here, ranking is governed by a
representation-stability property the consistency signal does not observe.
This is a narrower, more specific, empirically falsifiable -- and partly
falsified -- refinement of the original hypothesis that historical
consistency alone selects the right mechanism, not a confirmation of that
hypothesis.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.6

\subsection{The Production Case Study in Light of These Results}

The production observation described in Section~1.1 is consistent with
Section~5.2's positive finding -- a real deployment did show a measured
consistency statistic correlating with something real about its own data --
but it was never itself a controlled test of Section~5.3's ranking finding,
because production never ran a lexical-noise sweep of the kind this
experiment's VARIED condition applies. This experiment does not confirm the
production observation; it investigates the more general question that
observation raised. The production consistency figures cited in Section~1.1
remain, as stated there, generated by an earlier version of the measurement
pipeline and are cited from a confidential engagement, not independently
reproducible from this repository -- a caveat repeated here rather than
assumed to carry forward for a reader who begins at this section.

% - research/EVIDENCE_BASELINE.md Sec.1
% - research/r3_threshold_analysis.md

\subsection{What the Original Hypothesis Got Right and Wrong}

H1's deliberately weaker form -- better-than-chance agreement, not monotonic
dominance in every band -- is exactly why a PARTIALLY\_SUPPORTED verdict was
a coherent, pre-registered possible outcome rather than a post-hoc excuse.
The point estimate, 64.0\%, is directionally above chance in the only slice
where agreement is measurable at all (VARIED), and one full realized-ADS band
shows perfect, exceptionless agreement (32 of 32, Section~5.4), with the
corresponding by-nominal-target count independently significant at
$p=1.9\times10^{-9}$ (Section~5.6). This result is not without value, but it
is not evidence that H1 as originally intended was confirmed.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.18
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_EVIDENCE_CHECKPOINT.md Sec.9

The unconditional form of the original hypothesis -- that ADS thresholds
alone select the right mechanism -- is falsified, exceptionlessly, in the
realized $\geq0.90$ band. This is not a minor or marginal failure: it is the
exact band in which the frozen decision rule is most confident, since higher
realized consistency produces R3's strongest recommendation for rules, and it
is precisely there that the rule disagrees with the empirical winner in every
single one of 18 tested conditions.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.3 (C2b), Sec.4
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_FINAL_RESULTS.md Sec.6, Sec.8

\subsection{Mechanistic Explanation: Representation Stability}

The evidence is consistent with a specific mechanistic account. Realized ADS
is computed on the stable product identity, structurally blind to the
perturbable surface string; in this experiment, which mechanism wins is a
constant function of the lexical condition, a variable ADS cannot observe by
construction. We state this account explicitly as inferred from exhaustive
but post-hoc inspection of the frozen data and the generator's code, not as
the result of a second, independently designed confirmatory experiment: the
pattern holds without exception across all 240 rows, but a fresh, prospective
test of this exact causal claim on new data would strengthen it further
(Section~7.1).

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.2 step 8
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_DATA_DICTIONARY.md ("What realized ADS is, precisely")

Table~\ref{tab:t5}'s widening gap reveals a specific asymmetry: retrieval's
accuracy cost under lexical noise stays comparatively flat across realized-ADS
bands, while rules' cost grows with realized ADS. The perturbation degrades
an exact-match mechanism's precondition -- identical surface strings -- in a
way a fuzzy-matching mechanism is comparatively more robust to. We frame this
as a property of this specific perturbation model and this specific
retrieval implementation (rapidfuzz-based lexical similarity), not a claim
about fuzzy or embedding-based retrieval in general, which this experiment
does not test. This blindness is a direct consequence of how ADS is defined,
so the qualitative shape of 6b is partly built into the construction; what is
not given by that construction is the magnitude and direction actually
observed -- the empirical winner is constant across all 120 conditions of
each lexical condition (retrieval under VARIED, tie under CLEAN), and the
rules-minus-retrieval gap widens, rather than narrows, as realized ADS rises.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/EXPERIMENT_1_POSTHOC_ANALYSIS.md Sec.5-6
% - research/PAPER_CONTRACT.md Sec.6

\subsection{Relationship to Algorithm Selection and Meta-Learning}

This finding refines, rather than contradicts, the algorithm-selection and
meta-learning lineage \citep{rice1976,smithmiles2009,barbudo2023} by
identifying a specific failure mode of a single-feature, label-consistency-only
design-time selector: such a selector can be a good predictor of a
mechanism's own expected difficulty while carrying no information about a
separately governed ranking axis. Nothing here suggests that literature is
wrong or superseded, only that this narrow selector class has a specific,
empirically observed blind spot in this experiment.

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.5 row 2
% - research/literature/terminology_map.md

\subsection{Implications for Practice and Future Selector Design}

Four inferences this experiment does not license, stated explicitly rather
than left implicit. It does not follow that consistency-style signals are
useless -- the accuracy-prediction finding (Section~5.2) still holds. It
does not follow that this result generalizes to other lexical-noise models or
other retrieval implementations, neither of which was tested here. It does
not follow that a fix exists: no decision rule conditioning on both
consistency and a representation-stability signal was designed, prototyped,
or tested anywhere in this repository (Section~8.1). And it does not follow
that production data confirms this finding: production never ran the
lexical-noise manipulation this experiment depends on (Section~1.1,
Section~7.4).

% EVIDENCE:
% - research/PAPER_CONTRACT.md Sec.3, Sec.6

The natural next step this experiment motivates is a decision rule that
conditions mechanism selection on both realized consistency and a measured,
not assumed, representation-stability signal. We name this only as a
direction, in future tense, with no pseudocode, no proposed threshold values,
and no partial results, because none exists: presenting any implementation
detail beyond "condition on both signals" would misrepresent an untested idea
as a scoped method.

\subsection{H1 Only Partially Supported}

H1 (revised) is only partially supported, matching the pre-registered
PARTIALLY\_SUPPORTED row of the falsification table exactly -- stated here as
the honest verdict, not softened toward either "confirmed" or "refuted." The

\subsection{Synthetic Generator and Perturbation Model Scope}

This experiment uses a single synthetic generator family; its 240 conditions
replicate a seeded random-number generator, not an external population. The
lexical perturbation used ($P_{\mathrm{TRANSFORM}}=0.3$, five fixed transform
types) was pilot-tuned to a target corruption-share band, not derived from
measured real-world OCR or typo error rates. The practical-equivalence
margin ($\delta=0.02$) that defines a tie versus a defined winner
(Section~4.6) was likewise fixed once at calibration and never varied
against the frozen outcome data; because this margin is the specific reason
the CLEAN condition contributes zero defined comparisons (Section~5.6), this
paper cannot rule out that a different margin would yield defined
CLEAN-condition comparisons -- a sensitivity this paper does not test.

% EVIDENCE: research/CONTRIBUTION_LOCK.md Sec.9; research/EXPERIMENT_1_REDESIGN_REVIEW.md Sec.22

\subsection{Domain and Mechanism Scope}

The motivating case study is drawn from one domain, Romanian fiscal-document
invoice-to-account classification. The experiment itself is domain-agnostic
by construction (Section~3.1), but has not been run against a second
domain's data. Only exact-match rules and rapidfuzz-based retrieval were
compared -- not embedding-based retrieval, and not the shipped multi-tier
production cascade. The large-language-model mechanism was excluded from H1
for a documented, principled reason -- the synthetic product string carries
no signal predictive of the true label -- and was not evaluated at all in
Experiment~1.

\subsection{Production Data Limitations}

The production consistency figures cited in Section~1.1 (91.2\% deterministic
products, weighted ADS 0.847, unweighted ADS 0.964) were generated by an
earlier version of the measurement pipeline and remain likely understated and
unverified; they cannot be re-run against the corrected pipeline, since no
production data exists in this repository. Production never ran a
lexical-noise sweep; only two single-run data points feed the motivating
"R3 flip" narrative in Section~1.1. Production data does not independently
validate this experiment's finding, and this paper does not claim that it
does.

\subsection{No Selector Fix and Limited Generalization}

No decision rule conditioning on both realized consistency and a
representation-stability signal was designed, prototyped, or tested anywhere
in this repository; Section~8.1 names it only as a future direction. The
realized-ADS ceiling of approximately 0.91, a consequence of the fixed
cross-company-alignment parameter, means the region at or above roughly 0.93
was never reachable or tested by this generator -- untested, not merely
unfavorable. This paper makes no deployment or generalization claim: the
finding describes this experiment, not a general recommendation for how to
build classification systems.

\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper tested, with a pre-registered, 240-condition synthetic factorial
experiment, whether historical decision consistency predicts which of two
classification mechanisms -- exact-match rules or fuzzy retrieval -- should
be selected under a controlled lexical perturbation. Realized historical
consistency strongly predicts each mechanism's own accuracy. It does not
predict which mechanism wins: the unconditional form of the original
hypothesis is falsified, exceptionlessly, in the realized $\geq0.90$ band,
and H1 overall is only partially supported, not confirmed. The main lesson is
that historical consistency is informative about mechanism \emph{difficulty},
not mechanism \emph{ranking}, when, as observed here, ranking is governed by
a representation-stability property the consistency signal does not observe.
What remains open is whether a decision rule conditioning on both consistency
and a measured representation-stability signal would perform better -- named
here as a direction, not built or tested. This paper's contribution is a
precise, evidenced boundary on what a historical-consistency signal can and
cannot tell a system designer, not a solution to mechanism selection, not a
validated method, and not a new metric or architecture.
